How Much Do Labrador Owners Actually Feed? Real Cups-per-Day by Weight, Age and Activity

The vet formula behind every bag guide, what real Lab owners weigh out each day, and a calculator for your own dog.

Home›Blog›How Much to Feed a Labrador

If you have ever stood over a food bowl wondering how much to feed a Labrador while your dog stares at you like they haven't eaten in a week, you are in good company. Many Lab owners have heard a vet say "they're a bit heavy," felt a sting of guilt, and then been told to "just follow the bag," which can be a cup or two more than a real Lab seems to need. This guide puts the formula vets use next to what real owners report feeding, so you can find your own number without the shame.

Quick answer: Most adult Labradors need roughly 1,000 to 1,800 kcal a day, depending on ideal weight, neutering and activity. That works out to about 2.5 to 5 cups of dry food, but only once you know your food's kcal per cup, because the same "cup" can differ by about twofold between brands. Real owners in a Labrador forum thread fed their adult Labs anywhere from 140 g to 500 g of kibble a day. Start with the calculator below, then adjust every two to three weeks by what your hands tell you about your dog's ribs and waist.

The calculator uses the standard veterinary energy formula, your Lab's ideal weight and the calorie figure printed on your bag. It gives a starting range, not a prescription.

How much should you feed a Labrador per day?

A typical neutered adult Lab at a 65 lb ideal weight needs about 1,400 kcal a day by the standard formula, which is roughly 3.5 to 4.7 cups depending on the food. Many real Labs end up thriving on less.

Vets start with the resting energy requirement (RER), the calories a dog burns lying still. The Pet Nutrition Alliance calorie worksheet gives it as RER = 70 x (body weight in kg) to the power of 0.75, and then multiplies it by a life-stage factor: 1.6 for a neutered adult, 1.8 for an intact adult, 1.2 to 1.4 for an inactive or overweight-prone dog, and 2 to 3 for growing puppies. The same worksheet warns that individual animals can vary by as much as 50% from the predicted values, so the result is where you begin, not where you end.

Why the "right" amount is a range

Two Labs with the same weight can need very different amounts. Neutering, muscle, coat, climate, how much they swim and simply individual metabolism all shift the number. That is why the factors come as ranges, and why experienced owners talk about "feeding the dog in front of you."

Ideal weight, not current weight

For an adult, use the weight your dog should be. If your Lab is carrying a few extra pounds and you enter today's weight, the formula will happily keep them there.

How much do Lab owners actually feed?

Less than the formula suggests, in many homes, and the spread is wide. In one owner thread, adult Labs of roughly the same size were fed anything from 200 g to over 300 g of kibble a day, and owners judged success by how the dog looked, not by a number.

A 2019 thread called "Slim on 450g a day...." on the My Labrador Friends forum started when an owner was surprised that a visiting working-line dog was fed 450 g a day in two meals. Other members then posted what their own Labs eat. Most are UK owners who weigh food in grams:

First, owners cut portions when life changed: one reduced food by 25 g after walks were shortened, another halved a dog's ration while exercise was restricted. Second, several owners said they do not weigh food at all; one wrote that they "100% rely on what the dog looks like," adjusting as they go.

The puppy owner who trusted their eyes

Puppy owners face the same puzzle. In a 2018 thread on The Labrador Forum, the owner of a 7-month-old pup approaching 70 lb said the bag suggested 6 3/4 to 7 1/4 cups a day, which felt like too much because the dog was getting chubby. Another owner said their bag called for 3 to 3.5 cups for their puppies, and they fed "more like 2-2.5 cups." A third owner, whose pup was losing her waist on the pack amount, cut each meal by 10 grams at a time until the dog looked right again.

Keep in mind: forum replies are a small, self-selected group, and kibbles differ in calories per gram. These numbers show the real-world spread, not targets for your dog.

Why does my Lab act hungry all the time?

For about a quarter of Labs, it is partly genetic. A gene change makes them feel hungry again sooner between meals, so a begging Lab is not proof that you are underfeeding.

If you have been made to feel that a food-obsessed Lab is a training failure, this is worth reading slowly. A University of Cambridge study published in March 2024 looked at 87 adult pet Labradors and found that around 25% of Labs carry a mutation in the POMC gene. Dogs with it got hungry between meals more quickly, tried significantly harder in a "sausage in a box" test, and burned around 25% fewer calories at rest than dogs without it.

What that means at the bowl

A Lab like this can need fewer calories than the table suggests while begging harder than ever. The researchers suggest making a daily ration last longer: split it up, use puzzle feeders, or scatter it in the garden. In our calculator, answering "constant food-seeking" moves the estimate toward the lower, overweight-prone factor. A Lab who shadows you into the kitchen may also simply be a Velcro Lab who follows you everywhere, which is a separate habit from hunger.

How much should a Labrador puppy eat?

A growing Lab puppy needs about two to three times its resting energy, calculated on today's weight, so the amount climbs every few weeks as the pup grows.

The Pet Nutrition Alliance worksheet gives growth as 2 to 3 x RER. Younger, faster-growing pups sit nearer the top of that range and older pups nearer the bottom. Because a pup's weight changes so quickly, re-run the calculator every couple of weeks rather than setting one amount at 8 weeks and forgetting it.

Puppy food is not adult food

Feed a food labeled for growth and use that food's own kcal figure, since puppy and adult formulas differ. Growth is also the time when exercise questions come up; our look at the 5-minute-per-month puppy exercise rule covers what owners report there.

Does activity change how much a Lab should eat?

Yes, but less than most owners think. Moving from a couch Lab to an average one is roughly the jump from 1.3x to 1.6x RER, about 250 to 300 kcal a day for a 65 lb dog, or under one cup.

Very active Labs

The common worksheets stop at 1.8x for intact adults and do not give a single number for hard-working dogs. If your Lab runs, retrieves or swims daily, start at the top of the adult range and add food in small steps only while their ribs stay easy to feel. Swimming in particular burns energy; if you are building up to it, see our guide to teaching a Labrador to swim.

As Labs slow down

Many Labs settle down as they mature, and a calmer dog usually burns fewer calories. Our article on when Labradors calm down describes that shift; it is a good moment to re-check portions.

Cups, grams and calories: why "one cup" can mislead

A cup is a volume, not an amount of energy. Calorie density varies so much between foods that switching brands at "the same three cups" can quietly change your Lab's intake by hundreds of calories.

A study by Tufts veterinary nutritionists Deborah Linder and Lisa Freeman, published in the Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association in 2010, measured dry dog foods marketed for weight loss and found calorie densities from 217 to 440 kcal per cup. That is a twofold range within one category of food. A level cup also changes with kibble shape and with how you scoop, which is why many owners switch to a kitchen scale and weigh in grams.

Where to find your number

Look for the calorie statement on the bag, usually written as kcal/kg and kcal/cup. If only kcal/kg is given, weigh out a level cup once and do the math: grams per cup x kcal/kg / 1,000 = kcal per cup.

Step by step: find your Lab's real daily amount

Use this checklist once now and again whenever life changes: a new food, a new routine, neutering, or a slower season.

  1. Find the kcal per cup on your bag, or work it out from kcal/kg as above.
  2. Decide on an ideal weight for an adult, or use today's weight for a puppy.
  3. Run the calculator and take the middle of the range as day one.
  4. Subtract treats. Chews, training rewards and table scraps all count.
  5. Weigh the daily ration each morning, split it into meals and keep training treats out of that bag.
  6. Score the body every two to three weeks. On the WSAVA body condition chart, the ideal dog has ribs easily felt with minimal fat covering, a waist easily seen from above and a visible abdominal tuck. At a 7 of 9, ribs are felt only with difficulty under heavy fat cover.
  7. Adjust in small steps. Change portions by about a tenth at a time, the way forum owners trimmed 10 g to 25 g, and wait a few weeks before judging.
  8. Write it down. A note on the fridge with the date, grams and body check stops "a bit extra" from creeping in.

If you walk more than one Lab, keep each dog's ration separate even if their walks are shared; our guide to walking two Labradors covers the routine side.

Common feeding mistakes Lab owners make

  • Feeding the top of the bag range. Bag guides are written to cover many dogs. In the forum threads above, several owners fed well under them.
  • Scooping instead of weighing. A heaped cup and a level cup can differ a lot, every meal.
  • Forgetting the treats. 100 kcal of chews a day is real food for a dog eating 1,400 kcal.
  • Using current weight for an overweight adult. The formula then keeps the extra pounds.
  • Reading begging as hunger. Up to a quarter of Labs may be wired to feel hungry sooner. Slow the ration down instead of adding to it.
  • Not adjusting when life changes. Shorter walks, neutering or a calmer adult phase all lower needs.
  • Blaming yourself. A Lab who gained weight is not proof you failed. You now have a method, and small changes add up.

Frequently asked questions

How many cups of food should a 70 lb Lab eat?

By the standard formula, a neutered adult Lab at a 70 lb ideal weight needs about 1,500 kcal a day, which is roughly 3.7 to 5 cups of a food with 300 to 400 kcal per cup. Subtract treats, then adjust by body condition every few weeks.

Should I follow the feeding guide on the bag?

Use it as a starting point, not a rule. Bag guides cover a wide range of dogs, and many Lab owners in forum threads report feeding less than the bag suggests while keeping a visible waist. Your dog's ribs and waist are the final judge.

How many times a day should I feed my Labrador?

Most owners split an adult Lab's daily amount into two meals. Puppies usually get three or more meals, and forum owners describe moving to two meals around six months, mainly for convenience. The total for the day matters more than the number of meals.

My Lab is always hungry. Am I underfeeding?

Not necessarily. A 2024 University of Cambridge study found around 25% of Labradors carry a POMC gene mutation that makes them hungry again sooner between meals. Check ribs and waist: if they are easy to feel and see, your Lab is getting enough. Puzzle feeders and scattered kibble make meals last longer.

Should I feed my Lab by cups or by grams?

Grams are more accurate. Dry foods can range from about 217 to 440 kcal per cup, and scooping varies from day to day. Weighing the daily ration on a kitchen scale removes both problems.

Do neutered Labs need less food?

Usually, yes. The Pet Nutrition Alliance worksheet gives 1.6 x resting energy for a neutered adult versus 1.8 x for an intact one, about 11% less. In practice that is a small cut you confirm with regular body checks.

The bottom line

So, how much to feed a Labrador? Most adult Labs land somewhere between about 1,000 and 1,800 kcal a day, which is 2.5 to 5 cups of most dry foods, and real owners often feed less than the bag says. Work out your number from your food's kcal per cup, weigh it, count the treats and let your Lab's ribs and waist guide every small change. If you have struggled with this before, that is not a failure; it is just the starting point most Lab owners share.
