Why Your Labrador Keeps Taking Your Shoes and Parading Them Around: Carrying Habit, Trade Games and Better Greeting Items

Put the shoes away, hand over a better thing to carry, and make giving it up worth it.

Home›Blog›Labrador Taking Shoes

If your Labrador keeps taking your shoes and marching around the living room with one, you are watching a retriever with a mouth looking for a job and an audience. The good news is that you can usually redirect the parade instead of fighting it.

Quick answer (TL;DR): shut the shoes away, keep an allowed carry toy by the door, do not chase, and teach a calm trade so giving things up pays well. If your dog guards the shoe, get professional help. If part of it was swallowed, call your vet.

Why does a Labrador carry shoes around?

Carrying is something many Labradors are inclined to do, and a shoe is soft, handy and smells like you. What you do next can strengthen the habit.

Renee Rhoades, an applied animal behaviorist, told Adopt a Pet that breeds developed for hunting, including Labradors, Golden Retrievers and spaniels, are more likely to be predisposed to carrying items. She also names other reasons that can overlap: self-soothing during stress or excitement, wanting social contact, and simple learning, because bringing things has paid off before.

What genetics can and cannot tell you

A study of UK Kennel Club-registered Labradors published in Genetics used owner questionnaires to estimate how much of the variation in behavior traits is inherited. Fetching came out highest among the 12 main traits, with a pedigree-based heritability of 0.38 (standard error 0.08). The estimate based on DNA markers alone was much lower, 0.05.

Read that carefully. Heritability describes differences across a group of dogs, not how much of your own dog's behavior is "in the genes". The study measured fetching, not shoe theft. Still, it fits what owners see: some Labs are far keener than others to have something in their mouth, and training works with that rather than erasing it.

Why shoes in particular?

This part is our reasoning rather than research. Shoes sit at nose height near the door, they carry a strong scent of their owner, they are the right size to hold, and they reliably make people react. A cushion rarely gets three humans out of their chairs.

Is the shoe parade a greeting, a game or something else?

It depends on timing and body language. A loose, wiggly dog circling you at the door is greeting; a dog glancing back while darting off wants a chase; a stiff, still dog over an item may be guarding it.

Watch the whole dog before you decide. The same shoe can mean different things on different days.

The attention trap

Think about the last parade from your dog's side. They picked up a shoe, someone said their name, laughed, stood up and followed them. For a social dog, that is an excellent return on one second of work. Rhoades notes that praise, kisses or visible happiness can teach a dog to repeat the delivery. So the aim is to move the jackpot: lots of warmth for a toy, a quiet, dull swap for footwear.

How do you set up the lazy version of shoe protection?

Make the right object easier to reach than the wrong one. A closed cupboard, a rack behind a door and a toy basket at the entrance do most of the work before any training starts.

Five-minute setup

  1. Choose one place for shoes with a door or lid, and use it every time, guests included.
  2. Put a basket by the front door with two or three carry toys.
  3. Put a small jar of treats on a high shelf in the same area.
  4. Block the stairs or bedroom with a gate if shoes live there.
  5. Tell everyone in the house the same rule: toy gets a party, shoe gets a calm swap.

Choosing a better greeting item

Pick something your dog can hold comfortably with a closed or half-open mouth and parade proudly. It should be too big to swallow, easy to wash and in a good state. Check it often and bin it if pieces come loose.

Keeping the toy interesting

Keep the greeting toys mainly for arrivals and rotate them every few days. A toy that only appears when someone comes home stays more exciting than one that lies on the floor all week. If your dog's chosen object is a ball and they cannot let it go, start with our fetch rules and off switch.

How do you play the trade game step by step?

Start with a boring toy, offer something better, reward the release and give the toy back. Only later add shoes to real life, never as a deliberate practice object.

The sequence below follows the treat-lure-and-trade method described by Preventive Vet, which also warns that dogs who already guard items should not do it without a certified trainer.

  1. Load up. Have a few tasty treats ready and let your dog hold a toy.
  2. Show the treat. Put it right in front of the nose.
  3. Pay the release. When the toy drops, praise and give the treat.
  4. Return the toy. This shows that letting go does not mean losing it for good.
  5. Add the words. After several smooth repeats, say "drop it" and then present the treat hand.
  6. Wait a beat. Give your dog a second or two to process the cue.
  7. Fade the visible treat. Repeat until the words alone get the release, then reward.

A second method for dogs who clamp down

Preventive Vet also describes "charging" the cue, an approach popularized by behaviorist Chirag Patel. You say "drop it" and scatter treats on the floor, many times, while your dog has nothing in their mouth. Once the words make your dog light up and look for food, you start using them when they hold something. They spit it out to hunt for treats, and you calmly pick the item up.

A short practice plan

This pace is our suggestion, so slow down whenever your dog hesitates.

  • Days 1 to 3: a handful of trades with a dull toy, two short sessions a day.
  • Days 4 to 6: add the cue and use a toy your dog likes more.
  • Days 7 to 10: practice in the hallway, kitchen and yard.
  • After that: keep surprising your dog with a good reward now and then.

Trade, drop it or leave it?

They solve different moments. Trade or drop it is for something already in the mouth. Leave it is for the shoe your dog is looking at but has not picked up yet. Management covers every moment when you are not watching, which for most households is most of the day.

How can greetings stop ending in a stolen shoe?

Give your Lab the toy before they go shopping for a substitute. Cue "get your toy" as you come in, then greet once the toy is in their mouth.

Build the door routine

  • Come in with your own shoes headed straight for the cupboard.
  • Pick a toy from the basket and offer it low and calmly.
  • When your dog takes it, give the warm hello you would normally give.
  • Let them parade. You do not need to take an allowed toy back.
  • After a week or two, pause and point at the basket so your dog starts fetching it.

Guests can follow the same script. Hang a spare toy near the coat hooks and ask visitors to put shoes in the cupboard. If arrivals are chaotic in other ways, our guide on when Labradors calm down gives a realistic view of age and energy.

When the walk or the morning is the trigger

Some dogs grab footwear because shoes announce a walk. Break the link by touching your shoes at random times without going anywhere, and hand over the toy before you lace up. A dog that is already overexcited at the door often pulls once outside too; see when owners report leash pulling stops. Early-morning shoe deliveries to the bed are a close cousin of the 5 AM wake-up, and the same rule applies: do not pay it with a party.

When is shoe taking a safety problem?

It becomes urgent when something may have been swallowed or when your dog threatens you over the item. Both need a professional, not another game.

Swallowed pieces

The American College of Veterinary Surgeons explains that toys, leashes, clothing and other items can lodge in the stomach or intestines. Signs it lists include vomiting, loss of appetite, abdominal pain, dehydration and diarrhea, with or without blood. Strings are a particular concern because they can perforate the gut, so treat a missing lace seriously. Do not pull on string you see in the mouth or under the tail; call your vet.

Guarding signs

Stiffening, hovering over the item, a hard stare, lip lifting, growling or snapping all mean stop. Move children away, do not punish the growl and do not corner the dog. If the shoe is safe enough to leave for a minute, distance is your friend; if it is not, call your dog away to another room with something good and shut the door behind them. Then arrange help from a qualified reward-based trainer or behavior consultant, and ask your vet to rule out pain.

Which mistakes keep the shoe habit going?

Most of them make the shoe more exciting or leave it too easy to reach.

  • Chasing. It turns a carry into a game your dog is built to win.
  • Prying the jaws open. It can teach your dog to hold tighter or swallow fast.
  • Only trading for forbidden things. Practice with toys too, and give them back.
  • Calling your dog over to scold them. Coming to you should never be the bad part.
  • Inconsistent household rules. One person who plays keep-away undoes the plan.
  • Giving an old sneaker as a toy. Your dog cannot be expected to tell it from the new pair.
  • Training only mid-theft. Rehearse when everyone is calm.

Today's checklist

  • Shoes behind a door
  • Toy basket at the entrance
  • Treat jar within reach
  • One easy trade practiced
  • Whole household told the rule

What should you expect over the first two weeks?

Expect fewer chances for theft immediately, because the cupboard does that. Expect the new greeting habit and the cue to take longer and to wobble on exciting days.

Keep a simple note on your phone: date, what was taken, who was home and what happened next. After a week you will usually see a pattern, such as the hour before dinner or the arrival of one particular person. Aim your toy and your attention at that window. If a shoe still gets out, trade quietly, put it away and look at how your dog reached it. A slip is information about the setup, not defiance.

Questions owners ask about Labradors and shoes

Why does my Labrador keep taking my shoes?

Usually a mix of a retriever's liking for carrying things, excitement or a wish for attention, and the reaction the shoe gets. Shoes are also easy to reach.

Is my Lab bringing me the shoe as a present?

Nobody can say for sure. An applied animal behaviorist quoted by Adopt a Pet says we do not know that dogs understand gift-giving, though they likely learn that offering an object leads to attention.

Should I punish my Labrador for taking shoes?

We advise against it. Scolding or grabbing can teach your dog to run off or to hold on harder. Swap calmly and prevent the next chance.

How do I teach drop it?

Hold a treat near your dog's nose while they hold a toy, praise and treat when they let go, hand the toy back, then add the words drop it.

What if my dog growls when I try to take the shoe?

Do not take it by force and do not run trade drills alone. Ask a qualified trainer or behavior consultant for help.

What should I do if my Lab swallowed part of a shoe?

Call your veterinarian promptly. The American College of Veterinary Surgeons lists clothing among items that can lodge in the gut, with signs such as vomiting, loss of appetite and abdominal pain.

Will my Labrador grow out of carrying shoes?

Do not count on it. Chewing may ease with maturity, but carrying is a normal retriever habit, so give it an allowed outlet.

The short version

When a Labrador keeps taking your shoes, make the shoes boring and unavailable and make an allowed object the star of the greeting. Trade instead of chasing, give toys back during practice, and treat guarding or a missing piece as a reason to call a professional.
